Cells that wait their turn form islands in self-sorting arrays

When cells running different sorting rules share one array, cells of the same kind gather while it sorts. The gathering comes from when cells move, and it follows a waiting rule wherever we put it.

Levin, “Classical sorting algorithms as a model of morphogenesis: Self-sorting arrays reveal unexpected competencies in a minimal model of basal intelligence,” Adaptive Behavior, 2024, Available: https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05375 turned every number in an array into a cell that sees only its neighbours and swaps by its own rule, and the array sorts itself anyway. When they mixed cells running two different rules, cells of the same kind gathered into islands while the array sorted, and the authors suggested that cells with the more efficient rule settle first. We find that the islands come from waiting. Insertion cells wait until everything to their left is in order, and when we give that wait to Bubble cells they form islands almost as strongly as Insertion cells do, with the same number of swaps, while Insertion cells that never wait gather no more than chance. The islands dissolve once the array is sorted, unless one rule lets cells jump past others holding the same value. The original clustering result holds against a stricter test, and an array with frozen cells keeps sorting because a cell whose target is frozen moves on to the next one.

1Cells that wait their turn gather into islands

Take a row of a hundred numbers in random order and make each number a small cell that can see only its two neighbours. Give every cell a rule for when to swap places, let the cells act one at a time in no fixed order, and the row sorts itself, with no cell ever seeing the whole array and nothing in charge of the outcome. Zhang, Goldstein and Levin built this as a minimal model of morphogenesis, the process by which an embryo’s cells find their places in a body [1][1]T. Zhang, A. Goldstein, and M. Levin, “Classical sorting algorithms as a model of morphogenesis: Self-sorting arrays reveal unexpected competencies in a minimal model of basal intelligence,” Adaptive Behavior, 2024, Available: https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05375, because it asks the developmental question at a scale where every rule fits on a line: how much order can come from parts that each follow a local rule? Their code is open, and that is what made the rest of this report possible.

The cells borrow their rules from three textbook sorting algorithms. A Bubble cell picks a neighbour at random and swaps with it if the pair is out of order. An Insertion cell steps left past a larger neighbour, but only once every cell to its left is already in order, so it spends most of a run waiting. A Selection cell aims for the place it believes it belongs, starting at the left end, and jumps there directly, swapping with whoever holds a larger value there and aiming one place further on otherwise.

Now paint the cells by rule, half of them blue Bubble cells and half orange Insertion cells, shuffle them, and let the row sort. Partway through, the colours are no longer mixed at random: blue cells sit beside blue cells and orange beside orange, in short runs the paper calls aggregation and we will call islands, and once the row is sorted the islands dissolve again. Nothing in either rule refers to colour. So where do the islands come from?

The original paper suggested efficiency, with cells running the more efficient rule reaching their places first and the others catching up later, and it took the result further, predicting that a body built from cells of two kinds would form tissue-level islands. Their explanation already points at timing. We think the timing comes from one specific part of one rule, Insertion’s wait, and that the amount of work a rule does has nothing to do with it.

The picture shows what waiting does. In the left panel the orange cells mostly stand still, in vertical stripes, until the sorted stretch spreading in from the left reaches them, while the blue cells sort themselves around and between them, so the two kinds do their moving at different times. Give Bubble cells the same wait and the middle panel looks almost the same. Take the wait away from Insertion and the stripes break up, and with them any gathering beyond what randomly dealt colours would show.

Two more questions follow from the same picture. The islands vanish when the row finishes sorting, except in pairs that include Selection, whose long jumps can carry a cell past others holding the same value, which matters once values repeat. And when some cells are frozen and refuse to move, the array keeps sorting only if a cell whose target is frozen moves on to the next place, whether the cells run as parallel threads or one after another.

The islands are a product of schedules. Cells that move at different times end up beside their own kind for a while, and whether anything of that history survives into the finished array depends on whether cells can trade places with their equals.

2Same-kind cells gather more than shuffled colours would, as the original paper found

How do you know a run of three orange cells is an island and not luck? In a random half-and-half row about half of all neighbouring pairs already match in colour, and a sorting run passes through thousands of arrangements, so its best moment will beat one half by chance alone. Our yardstick replays each run with the colours dealt out again at random: the cells make exactly the same moves, and only the paint changes. If colour has nothing to do with how cells move, the real colours should do no better than the dealt ones.

Against that yardstick the original result holds. 3The wait makes the islands, and moving it moves them

What does Insertion do that makes its cells gather? Every rule here except Selection moves a cell one step at a time past an out-of-order neighbour, and they all make exactly one swap per out-of-order pair, so they do the same total work. What differs is when they do it. Insertion’s wait holds most of its cells still until the sorted stretch reaches them, which is the vertical striping in the left panel of Figure 2.

Delaying one kind of cell is enough to make it gather: a rule given the wait clusters, and Insertion without its wait does not. Timing is the main cause in this set of rules, and the Selection pairs show it is not the only one.

4Islands outlast the sort only when cells can jump past their equals

Now let the values repeat, with 100 cells holding the numbers 1 to 10 ten times each, as in the original’s second experiment. Two cells holding a 7 are like two people with the same ticket number in a queue: once the queue is in order, nothing says which of them stands first. The original found that clustering then lasts to the end, at 0.65 for Bubble with Selection and 0.7 for Insertion with Selection, and read this as support for the efficiency explanation, since Selection is the more efficient rule (p. 14).

We reproduce the lasting islands, and they come from Selection’s jumps. Insertion with Selection ends at 0.703 and Bubble with Selection at 0.690, both about 0.2 above the shuffles of the final arrangement. Bubble, Insertion and Gnome are stable, because they swap only strictly out-of-order neighbours. Selection is not.. Their finished row is therefore fixed by the starting shuffle, which ignores colour, and it ends at the shuffle level whatever the rules’ timing, after exactly as many swaps as the starting row has out-of-order pairs (2,259 on average). Selection’s jumps carry cells past their equals, no run containing Selection kept equal values in their starting order, and whichever cell claims a place first keeps it, so the timing of the two kinds gets written into the final row.

Timing makes islands that dissolve once the array is sorted. Only a rule that lets a cell jump past its equals can leave islands in the finished array, so the lasting clustering the original read as support for efficiency comes from Selection’s long jumps.

5An array with frozen cells keeps sorting when a blocked cell moves on

Freeze some cells, so that they hold their value and never start a swap. Can the others still sort around them? The original found that its cell-based sorting coped with frozen cellsA frozen cell never starts a swap. In the stricter version nobody can move it either; under the original default, active neighbours may still swap it. better than the traditional algorithms (p. 11). We asked which part of a rule carries that robustness, taking Selection apart along two lines: how far its swaps reach (anywhere, or only to a neighbour) and what a cell does when its target place is frozen (move on to the next place, or wait).

Moving on is what matters. When nobody can move a frozen cell, only far-reaching Selection that reroutesWhat a Selection cell does when the place it is aiming for holds a frozen cell: it advances its pointer and aims for the next place. The waiting variant keeps aiming at the frozen place. sorts the active cells, in all 30 runs at 3, 6 and 9 frozen cells out of 30, and with fewer swaps as fewer cells take part (108 swaps with none frozen, 56 with nine). Far-reaching Selection that waits stalls as soon as its target is frozen, and the neighbour-only variants can never carry a cell across a frozen one, so they keep swapping until the 5-second time limit (on average 14,077 swaps for neighbour-only rerouting and 6,831 for neighbour-only waiting with three frozen cells, against 412 and 404 with none). Under the original default, in which frozen cells cannot start a swap but active neighbours can still move them, far-reaching rerouting sorts 3 of 15 runs with one frozen cell and none with two or three, and every other variant fails whenever a cell is frozen. These outcomes follow almost directly from the rules, so we read them as checks that the implementation does what the rules say.

Running cells as parallel threads changes none of this. The original runs every cell as its own thread, but all threads share one lock, so only one cell acts at a time and the operating system picks which, which amounts to a random one-at-a-time scheduler. With frozen cells given no thread, threaded and one-at-a-time runs of the same rules make the same number of comparisons within about 3% (ratios 0.97 to 1.02) and succeed equally often. Neither Bubble nor Insertion reached a sorted array in any of the nine runs in either mode, so their ratios compare runs stopped at the time limit.

Two of the follow-up’s scores need reading with care. Far-reaching rerouting’s KA search-efficiency score: the base-10 logarithm of how much less effort the cells spend reaching order than a blind search through every reachable arrangement would spend. K = 30 means 10 to the 30 times less. falls from 30.45 to 18.01 as frozen cells go from 0 to 9, which looks like a loss of competence, but K stays 1.7 to 2.0 below the logarithm of the number of arrangements the active cells can take, so it falls because there are fewer arrangements to search and says little about how well the cells search. The dipsA stretch in which the array becomes less sorted before it recovers past where it started. The original paper reads these dips as a form of delayed gratification. reproduce, with Bubble dipping in every run (34.8 times on average) and Selection in 98% of runs (3.7 times), but nobody has yet asked how often a random walk with the same number of swaps would dip, so they cannot yet carry the original’s reading.

A cell whose target is frozen either waits, and the sort stalls, or moves on to the next place, and the sort finishes. That one choice decides whether the array survives damage, and running the cells as threads changes nothing about it.

6Four predictions that could prove the timing account wrong

None of these has been tested. Each names the result that would count against it.

6.1Islands should grow smoothly with the strength of the wait

We read the transplant as heterochrony in the developmental sense, where two cell populations following one global goal on different schedules leave temporary territories behind, and the territory reflects when a population acts rather than how much it does. A graded wait, in which a cell waits only for the nearest k cells to its left to be in order, should then raise clustering smoothly with k, and separation should predict the excess within a family of rules as well as between families. A graded wait that raises separation to the Insertion level without raising the excess, or an excess that jumps without a matching change in separation, would show that timing alone does not carry the effect.

6.2Lasting islands need a rule that lets equal cells trade places

Bubble with Insertion has timing differences and leaves no trace of them in the final row, because both rules are stable and the order among equal values is settled once, by the starting shuffle, while Selection settles that order as it goes. The account predicts that neighbour-only Selection (the neighbour-only rerouting variant above) leaves no final excess in a mixed array with repeated values, and that a far-reaching rule made stable, one that never jumps past an equal value, leaves none either; final clustering above the shuffles in either case would break it. Selection’s clustering also grows with array size, with Bubble with Selection rising from 0.026 at 30 cells to 0.052 at 50 and 0.073 at 100, while Bubble with Insertion stays between 0.06 and 0.08, which fits a mechanism tied to the length of Selection’s jumps. Those three sizes come from two implementations, so a single implementation at 200 cells in which Bubble with Selection does not exceed its 100-cell excess would weaken that reading.

6.3Robustness to damage comes from rerouting, on any substrate

Rerouting is a small, explicit form of the problem-solving the Levin program studies, a cell that reaches the same goal by another path when its usual one is blocked. With one shared lock the threaded runs cannot say whether a truly distributed substrate changes anything. Our bet is that a lock-free version, in which cells read stale neighbour values and conflicting swaps can collide, still lets far-reaching rerouting sort while the neighbour-only variants degrade further, and that cell-based sorting beat the traditional algorithms with frozen cells because those baselines lack rerouting. Neighbour-only rules recovering without a lock, or rerouting losing its advantage, would count against it.

6.4The tissue-islands prediction splits in two

The paper’s prediction that a body built from two kinds of cells will form tissue-level islands (p. 18) becomes testable in the model once each run is compared with shuffles of its own labels, and our results split it. Islands that form during rearrangement and then dissolve should appear whenever two cell types differ in when they move. Islands that last should need the types to share positional targets and at least one of them to move past equivalent cells. In a chimeric worm or a mixed-cell aggregate, the second version predicts that lasting islands go with exchanges among cells of the same positional identity, and lasting islands where such exchanges can be ruled out would show that the sorting model is missing a mechanism the tissue uses.

6.5Claims we have not rechecked

Several original experiments have no corrected counterpart yet: arrays in which the two kinds sort in opposite directions and settle into an incompletely sorted balance, the three-rule mixture, the efficiency comparisons between cell-based and traditional sorting, and the rise in dips with the number of frozen cells, which the original uses to argue that the behaviour is not a random walk and which needs the same kind of chance baseline as the clustering. The original also describes the algorithms as deterministic (p. 16), while Bubble picks its neighbour at random and the operating system orders the threads, so seeded threaded runs do not repeat exactly. Until these are redone, those claims stand as the paper reports them, without an independent check.

7Timing makes the islands and exchange keeps them

The self-sorting array is a small model organism with a rulebook we can read in full, and the principle of organization it shows here is a schedule. Cells of one kind gather because they move at a different time from their neighbours, and moving the waiting rule from one sorting rule to another moves the gathering with it, which makes the wait a handle on the pattern. What the finished array remembers of that history depends on whether cells can trade places with their equals: stable rules erase it and Selection’s long jumps write it into the final pattern, so the hidden variation that decides the outcome is the order among equal cells. In the lesion test, whether the array keeps its future under damage comes down to one rule, moving on past a frozen target, and not to the threaded substrate. The space of patterns this opens is small and structured, with graded waits, stable far-reaching rules and lock-free schedules each predicting where islands should form and whether they should last.

8Methods

The 100-cell and repeated-value results come from our own one-at-a-time reimplementation of Bubble, Insertion, Selection, Gnome and the wait variants, in which a uniformly chosen cell acts at each step; the separation, wait-transplant, frozen-cell and threading results come from the follow-up’s experiments on the original cell classes. Clustering is recorded after every swap, and curves from different runs are compared on a common axis of swaps completed. Every clustering result is scored against 200 shuffles of each run’s own labels, and intervals are 95% intervals over runs (mean ± 1.96 standard errors). Frozen cells run no thread. Table 1, Table 2 and Figure 3 use 100 cells and 40 runs per pair; the separation comparison 30 cells and 50 runs per pair (seed 20260306); the wait removal 50 cells and 20 runs (seed 20260324), and the wait transplant 50 cells and 30 runs pooled over seeds 40 to 42; the threading comparison 30 cells and 9 runs per condition; the Selection frozen-cell variants 30 cells. Figures 1 and 2 are illustrations, the second drawn from one seeded run per pair.

A.1What our earlier version got wrong

The earlier follow-up by Pouey and Rohr reran the original rules from byte-identical copies of the upstream cell classes and introduced the wait removal and transplant, the factorization of Selection’s frozen-cell robustness into the reach of its swaps and its rerouting, and the comparison of threaded with one-at-a-time execution. Rechecking it found four problems that changed numbers. Clustering was scored as peak share minus one half, and the highest point of a noisy curve sits well above one half even when colour has no effect, so most of each reported increase was chance; the earlier description of the drop without the wait as a sharp reduction understated it, because without shuffles the remaining raw value of 0.091 looked like residual clustering. Its Figure 2 sampled the arrays every 0.02 seconds of wall-clock time, which gave the slower waiting runs more samples and therefore higher maxima. The threaded runs in the work comparison started threads for frozen cells, which the original’s multithread_cell_sorting_with_frozen_steps.py does not do, although its freezing_sorting_analysis.py does, and a frozen Selection thread keeps counting comparisons without moving. The result files behind the figures had not been committed.

In one instrumented run with a single frozen cell (seed 1), 8,678 of 9,196 comparisons came from that frozen thread, while the active cells made 519 comparisons threaded and 531 one at a time.

A.3Independent checks

sim.py reimplements Bubble, Insertion, Selection, InsertionNoWait, DelayedBubble and Gnome one cell at a time, with a uniformly chosen cell acting at each step and the share of same-type neighbours recorded after every swap. null_n100.py runs 40 runs per pair at 100 distinct values (run seeds 5000 + t, shuffle seeds t) and duplicates_n100.py 40 runs per pair at values 1 to 10 repeated (run seeds 7000 + t); both use 200 label shuffles per run, and the duplicate check also scores the final arrangement against its shuffles and tests whether each group of equal values keeps its starting order. run_cluster.py is the earlier 50-cell check (excess 0.052 for Bubble with Selection, 0.075 for Bubble with Insertion). In the duplicate runs, Bubble with Bubble peaks 0.011 above its shuffles (0.003 to 0.020), a small residual we have not explained. rules_figure.py draws Figure 1, and islands_figure.py draws Figure 2 from run seed 5000 and prints each run’s peak and shuffle level on it. Run null_n100.py and duplicates_n100.py with uv run --no-project --with numpy==2.1.3 python <script> <out.json>, the two figure scripts with uv run --no-project --with numpy==2.1.3 --with matplotlib==3.9.2 python <script> <out.png>, and run_cluster.py with uv run --no-project --with numpy==2.1.3 --with scipy python run_cluster.py <directory holding sim.py> <out.json>.
