Orbium is a Lenia creature that glides across its grid while the values under it keep changing, so its shape lasts only as long as its rule keeps rebuilding it. We handed a search one frame of it at step 600, the kind of rule and the starting cells, hid the three numbers that set the rule’s reach and growth, and the search came back within six percent of the native value on each, with a body that stays whole for another 600 steps it was never scored on. Reading a rule out of a body is the easy end of something we want, which is to write down a body and have a search find a rule that grows it and keeps it. Two larger cohorts show what such a target already carries. Across 8,155 Flow Lenia creatures, shape explains about half of how a body travels, nearly as much as how active it is. Rules that grow the same shape behave more alike than random rules, yet at six of seven starting rules they still differ in how the body moves and how it answers a cut, by more than one rule varies on its own, and some of those differences leave no trace in the body we can measure. Across 160 creatures cut at their centre, complexity says almost nothing about repair.

1One frame of Orbium was enough to recover its rule

OrbiumNative Orbium (top) and the body the recovered rule grows (bottom), each frame centred on its body, from step 100 to step 1,200.A Lenia creature first described by Chan: a crescent-shaped body about twenty cells across that glides in a straight line while the values under it keep changing, so its shape lasts only as long as its rule keeps rebuilding it. is the best-known creature of Lenia, the continuous cellular automaton in which each cell grows or decays according to a smooth weighted sum of its neighbourhood [1][1]B. W.-C. Chan, “Lenia: Biology of artificial life,” Complex Systems, vol. 28, no. 3, pp. 251–286, 2019, doi: 10.25088/ComplexSystems.28.3.251.. Its rule is a kernel, a growth function and three numbers: the interaction radius RThe kernel radius: how far, in cells, a cell’s neighbourhood reaches. Orbium’s native value is 13., the growth centre mThe growth centre: the neighbourhood density at which a cell grows fastest. Orbium’s native value is 0.15. and the growth width sThe growth width: how far the neighbourhood density can stray from m before growth turns into decay. Orbium’s native value is 0.017.. We gave a search the update law, the kernel shape, the growth function and Orbium’s starting cells, and hid those three numbers. Each candidate was scored by growing it from the same cells for 600 steps and comparing its whole field with the reference at step 600 (the field matchOne minus half the summed absolute difference between the candidate’s mass field and the reference, both scaled to unit mass and aligned at their centres of mass, minus small penalties for differences in total mass and in the number of occupied cells. A perfect match scores 1.). The search started from R = 10, m = 0.12 and s = 0.024, a rule that breaks the seed into scattered rings, and it ran 2,688 candidates across the full parameter ranges and then 1,152 more around the best region.

It came back with R = 12.94 (native 13), m = 0.1526 (native 0.150) and s = 0.0180 (native 0.017), within six percent of the native value on each number. The broad stage had already put the radius and the growth centre close, and the narrow second stage raised the field match from 0.878 to 0.969 while pulling the growth width in from 0.0194. The body the recovered rule grows stays coherent through step 1,200, twice the horizon it was scored on.

More of the history did not help. A control search scored on steps 100, 300 and 600 with the same two-stage budget returned R = 12.80, m = 0.1435 and s = 0.0185, each farther from the native value than the single-frame fit, although its body also stayed coherent through step 1,200. This is one organism and one control, so it does not show that single frames are better targets in general, only that here the two earlier frames gave the search nothing it could use.

For someone trying to specify a body, this is the easy end of the problem. When the kind of rule and the starting cells are known, one picture of the mature body pins its rule down, and the body that rule grows keeps going on its own past the moment it was checked. The rule family, the seed and the elapsed time were all handed to the search here, and a body we invent will come with none of them.

2Shape predicts how a body travels nearly as well as how active it is

A body that matches a requested shape could still move in any number of ways, so we measured six behaviours in 8,155 Flow Lenia creatures from one screen of 8,192 seeds and reduced them to principal axes. W.-C. Chan, “Lenia and expanded universe,” in Proceedings of the 2020 conference on artificial life (ALIFE 2020), MIT Press, 2020, pp. 221–229. doi: 10.1162/isal_a_00297., so their bodies glide, spin, pulse and break apart in ways a one-channel Lenia rule rarely does. Size and elongation carry most of it. If you write down a body by its size and outline, you have already chosen much of how it will move. Shape leaves nearly half of the variation in travel unexplained, which is where an unusual gait would have to come from, and a search scored on shape alone has no reason to look there.

3Rules that grow the same body still differ in how it moves and heals

Shape fixes about half of how a body travels across a whole screen, and the other half could come from two places: from differences of form a screen-wide fit cannot resolve, or from the rule acting on behaviour through channels that leave the form alone. To separate them we held shape fixed. Around Flow Lenia rule 2300 from a one-channel corpus we searched nearby rules and kept 48 shape-matesRules that grow the starting rule’s shape to within the smallest shape change a tiny random change of that rule produces. Each starting rule has 48 of them, chosen as far apart from each other as the search allowed., rules that grow the same body to within the smallest change a tiny random nudge of the rule produces, and paired each with a rule at the same distance in a random direction with no shape requirement. Every mate and neighbour was grown from the same eight starting fields, once intact and once with its centre cut away halfway through its 1,600 steps, and we measured how far it travelled, how fast, how much energy its field carried and how far its form drifted after the cut. One rule grown from 64 starting fields gives the yardstick, its own scatterHow much one rule’s behaviour varies when the same rule is grown from different starting fields, measured over 64 fields and scaled to the eight fields each shape-mate was grown from.. The 48 mates of rule 2300 differ in behaviour by 5.2 times that scatter.

The same holds at six of seven starting rules, at 1.6 to 5.2 times the scatter. At the seventh, rule 1643, whose neighbourhood is the flattest we found, mates differ by less than one rule varies on its own, and an eighth starting rule yielded too few mates to measure. Yet the mates still behave more alike than neighbours at the same distance in rule space, in every behaviour at every starting rule, spreading between 0.06 and 0.97 times as much (median 0.43). Holding shape fixed narrows behaviour without fixing it.

What carries the differences depends on the behaviour. Energy follows form: at five of the seven starting rules the twelve shape descriptors, measured on four starting fields, predict 55 to 91 percent of the mates’ energy differences on the other four, so mates that pass as the same shape still differ in it slightly, and their energy reads those differences. Speed and the response to the cut do not. The speeds of the mates of rule 2300 are as reproducible across fields as their energies, and no description of the body we have, its outline or its interior, predicts more than a quarter of them. At rule 2221 how far the form drifts after the cut is reproducible to a split-half correlation of 0.96, nothing in the body predicts any of it, and the mate’s offset in rule space predicts 64 percent.

Across whole rule families the hold of shape on the rule is weaker still. Among a quarter of a million random two- and three-channel rules per family, bodies with the most similar shapes come from rules 0.997 to 0.999 as far apart as random pairs, about one percent of the closeness the sample allows. The uncorrected engine gives the same ratios on the same rules, so the bug did not create that looseness.

Two rules that grow the same body can make it move or heal differently, and for some of those differences nothing in the body tells them apart while the rule does. A target written as a picture of the body leaves that part of the rule open.

4More complex bodies do not recover better from damage

A body we can specify also has to survive disturbance, and an intricate body does not survive it any better. We ran 40 coherent creatures and 120 random rules from the same starting patch, cleared a 40 by 40 square at the centre of each at step 400, and measured how far occupancy, spread and the largest piece’s share of mass ended from an undamaged copy by step 1,000, which we call form driftThe mean relative difference between the cut creature and an undamaged copy in occupancy, radius of gyration and the largest piece’s share of mass, over the steps from the cut at 400 to step 1,000. Zero means the cut left no trace on these measures.. ComplexityHow poorly the body’s mass map compresses: the compressed size of the image relative to its raw size, averaged over four downsampling scales. Intricate bodies score high, smooth or empty ones low. is the multiscale structural measure from the same runs.

What little link there is points the wrong way: the more complex a body, the slightly further it drifted (r = 0.16 across the 160). The coherent creatures were more complex than the random rules and drifted further after the cut (0.34 against 0.25 on average), and only 31 of the 160 landed in the high-complexity, low-drift corner.

An intricate body is no more likely to heal. If the thing you want has to survive being cut, the target has to say so, with the cut inside the test that scores it.

5The next search has to find the seed as well as the rule

Orbium was recovered with its starting cells handed over, and a body we invent arrives without them. The cohorts add what any such target carries with it: its size and elongation already decide much of how it moves, the rules that grow it can still differ in how it moves and heals, and its complexity says little about whether it will repair damage.

Our bet is that a search over seed and rule together can start from a compact patch, grow a withheld target body, and keep it, so that the route into the body is found along with the dynamics that hold it. The test hides a creature’s rule and seed, searches both against one frame of the mature body, then changes the starting conditions and cuts the fitted body. The bet fails if the rules that match the frame reach it only from the one seed they were found with, or if the fitted body drifts after the cut further than the native creature does, because then a frame pins down a picture and leaves the body that should keep it unspecified.

If it holds, specifying a body means handing over a picture of it and the damage it has to survive, and letting the search work out where to begin.

6A body is the target of a rulebook

We ask whether different kinds of systems share principles of organization: how a body takes shape, how it holds that shape and what it does when something is taken away. Lenia is a model organism for this because it comes with its rulebook, and this report runs the question backwards, from a body to the rulebook that grows it. For Orbium, with the kind of rule and the seed handed over, one frame is enough, and the rule it yields keeps the body going long after the frame it was read from. A body, here, is something the rulebook aims at and holds, and one picture of that body says enough about the aim to recover the rule.

The cohorts show how much more a target has to say once nothing is handed over. Shape fixes about half of how a Flow Lenia body moves and leaves the rest to hidden variation a picture does not show. Rulebooks that grow the identical body share much of their behaviour, which suggests that shape and behaviour read a few of the same organizing combinations of the rule, and the lesion assay, does the body keep its future, still tells some of those rulebooks apart when their bodies look the same. Complexity tells us nothing about that question. A target that should survive damage has to carry the damage inside its own test.

Appendix

A.1Engine correction and rerun

Earlier versions of this work reported results from Flow Lenia simulations whose spatial-derivative step computed the gradient with its sign flipped and its axes swapped, in a Python port of the simulator and in our main engine before it was fixed. Under that step the median creature broke into twelve pieces, which decoupled how bodies travelled from their shape. Those results are removed, and the shape and movement analysis and the damage assay in this report were rerun on the corrected engine, LeniaCLI build 060e90a71ec1, with the analysis code unchanged and the harvest regenerated from the same rules and seeds.

A.2The withdrawn specimen search

A search that tried to recover the rule of corpus specimen 2876 is withdrawn. Its batched initializer grew each candidate from a different random starting field, so the apparent improvement over generations belonged to the starting fields and not to the rules.

A.3Orbium and its replay

Orbium runs in additive Lenia, a single channel on a 192 by 192 torus, with the native rule Chakazul O2(a) from Chan’s Lenia library [1][1]B. W.-C. Chan, “Lenia: Biology of artificial life,” Complex Systems, vol. 28, no. 3, pp. 251–286, 2019, doi: 10.25088/ComplexSystems.28.3.251.. Additive Lenia has no derivative step, so the correction above does not touch it, and replaying the native, broad-stage and recovered rules on the corrected engine reproduced all 900 recorded frames byte for byte. Both searches are evolution strategies with 96 candidates per generation, 28 broad generations over R in 9 to 17, m in 0.1 to 0.2 and s in 0.01 to 0.03, then 12 narrow generations around the best candidate. The three-frame control sums the field match over steps 100, 300 and 600, so its best score of 1.08 is not comparable with the single-frame score of 0.969.

A.4Shape-mates

The eight starting rules come from the one-channel corpus behind this work, each a point in a 40-coordinate parameter box. Around each we sampled rules at distances from 0.005 to about 0.35 and accepted those whose twelve shape descriptors, averaged over four selection fields, lay within a tolerance of the starting shape. The tolerance is the median shape change produced by 24 random rule changes of size 0.005, which ranges from 0.003 standardized shape units around rule 1643 to 0.28 around rule 2876. From the accepted rules the 48 farthest apart were kept, and each was paired with a neighbour at its exact rule distance in a random direction. Mates and neighbours were grown from eight measure fields for 1,600 steps, with and without a central cut at step 800, on LeniaCLI build 060e90a71ec1, and each starting rule from 64 replicate fields. Rule 3029 yielded 10 acceptable rules of 1,120 screened, against a minimum of 12, and was not measured.

For each of six log behaviours (displacement, speed, tortuosity, energy, variance and form drift after the cut) the spread ratio divides the spread of the 48 mates’ eight-field means by the standard error of an eight-field mean of the starting rule, and the per-rule value is the geometric mean over the locomotion, energy and response domains. The design was frozen before the runs: a starting rule supports the conjecture that mates differ beyond scatter when the lower 95% bound exceeds 1 and falsifies it when the upper bound falls below 1.25, and the conjecture was to be judged on six supporting or four falsifying of eight. With 3029 unmeasured we judge it on the seven measured rules, where six support and one falsifies; counting 3029 as a failure among eight gives the same outcome. The frozen bootstrap resampled each mate’s fields independently although all mates share them, so the intervals shown keep the fields paired; no verdict changes. Form predictions are ridge regressions across the 48 mates scored on held-out fields, from the shape descriptors, from a heading-free description of the body’s interior, and from both. The family-wide comparison uses four families of 28 runs of 8,192 random rules each, grown once for 1,500 steps on a 128-cell world on both engines from the same seeds, with rule distances measured after sorting kernels and also minimized over every kernel and channel relabelling.

A.5Measurements

The six behaviour measurements are mean speed, path length, displacement and centre velocity (each log-transformed) and the field’s temporal variance and energy, standardized before the principal axes are taken. The creatures are those of the screen that passed its persistence filters. Each shape fit is an ordinary least-squares regression of one behaviour measure or axis on the eight standardized shape measurements; the size and elongation groups are fitted the same way on their own subsets. Form drift and complexity are defined in their notes; the cut is a 40 by 40 square set to zero at the centre of the grid at step 400.

A.6Data

Every number and figure here is produced by the report’s figure script from the recorded runs: the two-stage Orbium inverse with its configs, histories and frames, the corrected-engine shape and movement regeneration with the harvest it read, the shape-mate runs with their frozen analysis and its controls, the family-wide rule-distance analysis, and the corrected-engine damage assay with the Orbium replay. The source manifest lists each of these files with its size and SHA-256, and the generated figures with theirs. The figure summary holds the recomputed values behind the prose: the recovered rule numbers and their errors, the explained variances and fits, the shape-mate spread ratios, and the damage correlation and counts. The Orbium inverse summary records the native, starting and recovered rules of both searches with their scores.
