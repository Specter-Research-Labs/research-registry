Can we recover the rules of a Lenia creature?

We asked a search algorithm to reproduce a tapered Lenia body. It found a segmented one with similar measurements. The mismatch exposes a difficulty in designing living-looking forms: what must we specify for a rule to grow the body we actually want?

5,000-rule corpus + specimen 2876 and Orbium reruns · rebuilt 2026-09-02

The desired body keeps its form through a continuous process of local change. Recovering it means finding dynamics that sustain the anatomy, not merely an image that resembles it. We approached that problem with three different requests. A search scored on four measurements exposed what those measurements leave out. Supplying a complete body let us search for a rule that maintains it. Fixing a known rule family let us recover hidden parameters from an observed frame.

01

A better score produced a different body.

Specimen 2876 was selected from the 5,000-form corpus because its developed body is compact, tapered, and easy to recognize. The search receives four measurements from its 1,200-step trajectory. It does not receive the target images.

The fitted organism reproduces several bulk measurements, yet its anatomy is visibly different. The segmented structure shows how much body-plan variation remains when the target specifies only four measurements.

We excluded specimen 2876 itself, retrieved twelve rules with nearby measurements, and refined the best proposal through six generations of 32 simulations. The search cost fell from 6.99 to 5.26. The bodies below show what that improvement actually bought.

Inspect the fitted rule and the implementation comparison

The MLX search cost and the separate Swift replay cost of 0.975 use different forward implementations. They are not successive points on the same improvement curve. This is one leave-one-out target.

02

We searched for a rule that could maintain a supplied body.

The segmented result suggests giving the search the spatial information its score omitted. Here we supply a complete body from the dataset as the initial field. The search must find a rule that keeps that field coherent, active, and topologically similar during the recorded rollout. It can now test how to sustain the anatomy without also having to discover how to grow it.

03

One mature frame was enough to recover nearby Orbium parameters.

Orbium lets us ask how much a mature body reveals when we already know the kind of rule that made it. We supplied its update law, kernel, growth function, and starting cells, then hid three numerical parameters: the interaction radius R, growth center m, and growth width s. Each candidate develops from those same starting cells and is compared with the reference body at step 600.

Fix the model

The search uses the native qd24_additive_v1 update and Orbium's one-kernel geometry. Other update laws and kernel layouts are not considered.

The search starts from R=10, m=0.12, and s=0.024. These values disperse the initial cells into a ring and small fragments.

We repeated both stages with three frames

A control search used steps 100, 300, and 600. It returned R=12.80, m=0.1435, and s=0.0185. Its replay also remained coherent through step 1,200.

The single-frame run recovered closer values

Both runs used the same broad and refinement budgets. The three-frame run ended farther from the hidden values than the run scored only at step 600. This is one organism, so it does not establish that single-frame targets are generally better.

04

Nearby body measurements can come from distant rules.

The reconstructions raise a question about the whole collection: how many different rules can fit a similar body? We represent each body by its measured phenotype and call the set of compatible rules a fiber. If that set is broad, choosing a measured form still leaves considerable freedom in the dynamics that produce it.

The 19.6 and 37.7 dimension estimates answer different questions. The first compares the global intrinsic dimensions of the 5,000-rule corpus and its 15 measured outcomes. The second subtracts the effective rank of a seven-descriptor local response from the full 40-parameter rule. Both indicate substantial ambiguity and should not be compared numerically.

05

How reliably does the search improve a target?

Retrieving a nearby rule gives the search a starting point; running it reveals whether that proposal produces the requested measurements. Earlier tests examined both parts: whether proposed rules produce stable bodies, and whether simulation-guided refinement improves their match across several targets.

The results below test parts of this system. The conditional proposal check used eight samples across two targets. The refinement result used four targets. The MLX and Swift comparison covered one rule, one starting field, and six steps.

The conditional model produced stable bodies but missed the target measurements.

1A1B1C1D2A2B2C2D

All eight simulations remained stable. Their mean descriptor error was 2.79 standardized units, where zero is a perfect match, and an average of 3.6 parameters per sample had to be clamped back into range. This is a two-target mechanism check, not an accuracy estimate for the full target population.

06

Shape predicted activity far better than locomotion.

A body that matches the requested shape may still move differently. To examine that gap, we measured six behaviors in 8,174 creatures and reduced them to principal axes. The first axis is dominated by speed, path length, displacement, and center velocity, so we call it locomotion. The second is dominated by energy and temporal variance, so we call it activity. Together they account for 87.2% of the behavioral variation.

ActivityR² 0.725
LocomotionR² 0.038
TortuosityR² 0.110

Occupancy and spread helped predict activity. The same shape measurements explained only 3.8% of the locomotion axis. Matching form measurements does not determine how the result will move.

07

Endpoint density was a poor guide to developmental motion.

If many trajectories finish in the same region, it is tempting to picture development as rolling downhill toward it. We tested that picture by fitting a potential surface to the endpoints and comparing its downhill direction with the observed motion. The alignment was weak. In a separate test, five of eight genotypes became more dispersed after their starting conditions were perturbed. Frequent endpoints therefore did not supply the restoring dynamics a compiler would need to rely on.

Bulk shape was much more repeatable than motion across starting conditions. Across the same six initial conditions, mean variation was 2.5% for shape and 51% for motion. A rule can produce a broadly consistent form while leaving its trajectory highly contingent on the start.

08

Can the search discover how to grow the body?

The three searches solve different parts of the problem: matching measurements, maintaining a supplied field, and recovering parameters within a known family. None yet discovers both a compact seed and a rule that reliably grows an arbitrary requested body.

The next test should require the search to discover a developmental route: start from a compact seed, grow a withheld target body, and maintain its anatomy. Searching for the seed and rule together would address the part that the supplied-field experiment leaves open. Changed starting conditions and damage would then test whether the fitted dynamics keep producing and sustaining the requested body beyond the trajectory used to select them.
