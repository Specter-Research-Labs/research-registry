Does measuring the whole help us change it?

An organization score may anticipate development without predicting recovery or selecting a better intervention. Prediction, target achievement, and an advantage over simpler controllers need separate tests.

Does the score help predict recovery?

If a measure of organization tells us something useful about a body, we would like to know whether taking that measurement before an injury helps us predict how the body will recover. We tested one candidate, ΦR, which compares predictive information in the whole system with that remaining under a partition, on the 211 trajectories with complete features and binary outcomes. Its association with recovery changed direction across the three families, and adding it did not establish an improvement on held-out runs: the estimated change in prediction loss was +0.00479, with a 95% interval from −0.00189 to +0.01205 (lower loss is better).

A single ordering from less to more organized would be especially useful if moving upward told us which bodies could recover. Here the association changes direction between families, so the same numerical increase does not have a consistent relationship with that capacity. One possible explanation is that recovery depends on a particular arrangement of interactions that the scalar combines with other, less relevant sources of predictability. This is an interpretation of the failed prediction, not something the regression identifies. It also matters which quantity is used: ΦR, the whole-minus-sum score used elsewhere in this campaign, and causal effectiveness at micro and macro scales ask different questions.

We encountered a related difficulty when using whole-state feedback to steer the body: it moved the body toward a target, but restoring an omitted sham comparator left its advantage over the strongest tested alternative unresolved, and after release that relative advantage reversed at the held-out endpoint. Reaching the target while feedback is active therefore leaves us with two further questions, whether whole-state observation adds anything necessary and whether the induced change persists once control stops. The controllers also consulted simulated candidate futures when choosing their actions, which is part of the assistance available to these control procedures.

Prediction and intervention test different explanations

Several earlier reports find that whole-field organization rises before a visible formation event. Temporal precedence makes a measure a candidate predictor; changing that measure and obtaining a better outcome is a further claim. Adaptive interventions complicate the inference because selecting a high score also selects a particular physical action. The action may matter for reasons the score does not capture.

Giving the alternative observers the same intervention budget and targets lets us ask what their different observations contribute, provided the comparison also includes doing nothing (the omitted sham changed the controller tournament’s verdict). We then need to follow what happens after steering stops, measuring both how much of the target is retained and whether the controller still has an advantage over its alternatives, since losing that advantage need not mean losing every effect of control.

Choosing an intervention before seeing its outcome

The ambition is to find a description of organization that tells us how to act. If two bodies receive the same score but require different interventions to recover, the distinction we need lies somewhere the score has collapsed. A collection of measured responses could retain it, giving each body a description in terms of what it can resist, recover from, or become. The controller comparisons add a further demand: such a description should help select an action on an unfamiliar case, with the same action budget and access to simulated futures as its competitors. The useful abstraction would earn its place by making those choices better.
