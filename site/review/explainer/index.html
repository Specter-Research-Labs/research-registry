What kind of thing are we mapping?

A Lenia creature is a pattern maintained by repeated local updates. Each location samples its surroundings through a kernel; a growth function turns that neighbourhood measurement into an increase or decrease in activity. Changing those functions can change which bodies form and how they move (Chan 2019).

Flow Lenia changes the accounting: in its mass-conserving formulation, the update transports matter rather than creating or deleting it (Plantec et al. 2025). Conservation alone does not keep a body intact. The same total matter can remain concentrated, separate into fragments, or spread across the field. Preserving matter and preserving an individual are different achievements.

Our morphology map compresses a snapshot into twelve measurements. It records aspects of proportion, symmetry, coverage, and fragmentation. It does not contain the complete state or the rules that produced it. Two nearby points can therefore hide different dynamics. The question is whether this compressed description retains enough information to predict something beyond appearance.

A space of possibilities, with a testable structure

Michael Levin’s Ingressing Minds proposes studying a structured space of possible patterns spanning anatomy, physiology, and behaviour. He argues that changing an embodiment can expose patterns outside its familiar repertoire; his broader explanation invokes non-physical patterns that inform physical systems (Levin 2026, secs. 3, 4.2, and 5.4).

That proposal motivates a concrete question here: which similarities survive a change of rules or circumstances? Our measured shape space is an empirical construction, not an identification of Levin’s proposed Platonic space. We can nevertheless test whether its neighbourhoods tell us something useful about what bodies can do.

The first comparison is deliberately modest. A group selected using only Flow Lenia geometry turned out to be closer to measured fish shapes. “Selected without fish data” describes that selection step; it does not mean these patterns arose without search or selection of any kind. The coordinate-by-coordinate comparison below shows what the resemblance consists of—and why a close numerical neighbour need not look convincingly fish-like.

A body is also a history

A snapshot hides the work of maintaining a pattern. In the replays, look for the continued organization of a moving body, not simply a recognizable silhouette in one frame. The distinction matters when we later measure forward progress, turning, and connected-body coherence separately.

This is where self-maintenance becomes a useful concept. Flow Lenia research connects persistent localized patterns to the artificial-life literature on autopoiesis and constitutive autonomy (Plantec et al. 2025, introduction). Those concepts ask how an organized system produces and sustains itself. A persistent replay gives us a starting observation; it does not, by itself, establish all the stronger biological or cognitive properties associated with those theories.

Likewise, an attractor means a dynamical regime approached from a range of initial states. Seeing one body persist along one trajectory is insufficient to establish that range. Perturbations would let us investigate it: does the body return to a comparable regime, settle into another, or lose its organization?

The prediction worth testing

An earlier shape-to-behaviour analysis already found a pronounced difference: shape measurements predicted an activity axis with R² = 0.725, but a locomotion axis with R² = 0.038. These were descriptive regressions on 8,174 creatures, not a test of obstacle response. They give the next experiment a starting point: the usefulness of shape depends on which behaviour we ask it to explain.

Our hypothesis is that morphology will predict some response differences, but similar-looking bodies maintained by different dynamics will sometimes respond differently to the same intervention. This is a proposed experiment, not a conclusion from the five-body assay.

A useful test would pair nearby bodies in the measured shape space, including pairs from different rule families. At matched phases of their movement, apply comparable obstacles and compare their response with an unobstructed continuation. Measure progress, heading, fragmentation, and recovery over time. Then ask whether adding rule information improves prediction on bodies from families excluded during fitting.

If shape alone generalizes across those families, the map captures a regularity broader than the individual rules. If rule information materially improves prediction, the same morphology conceals different ways of maintaining and moving a body. If neither generalizes, we should revisit what our twelve measurements omit, including temporal phase and internal organization.

There is another neighbourhood to investigate: bodies reachable through small changes in a generating rule. It need not coincide with the neighbourhood defined by similar appearance. Comparing the two would give experimental substance to the question of nearby possibilities raised by Levin’s programme (Levin 2026, sec. 5.4).

Where these questions lead

Levin’s references offer several routes into this history: D’Arcy Thompson’s On Growth and Form (reference 102), Webster and Goodwin’s Form and Transformation (76), Waddington’s The Strategy of the Genes (178), and Thom’s Mathematical Models of Morphogenesis (248). These are further-reading leads from his bibliography, rather than independent evidence for our results. Their distinctions—geometric transformation, generative process, developmental trajectory, and stability—are precisely the distinctions our figures should make visible.
