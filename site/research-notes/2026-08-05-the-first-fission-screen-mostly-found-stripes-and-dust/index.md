# Stripes, dust, and false fission

_5–8 August 2026 · Negative pilot result._

It was dust. The one fission event our first screen found had a second body holding 0.060 units of mass, in a system of about 276.6.

We were looking for organisms that genuinely divide, one coherent Flow Lenia body becoming two substantial bodies that stay apart, because a planned causal-emergence experiment depended on them. A conservative screen of 24 runs produced no split in which the descendants kept between 70 and 130 per cent of the parent’s mass and stayed separate for 50 steps, and a more active condition with 64 patches also settled back into one dominant body.

A different failure appeared on striped organisms. A fixed activity threshold sliced one continuous body into dozens or even hundreds of components because the dim gaps between stripes were being mistaken for empty space.

We repaired that part of the test by judging components relative to the parent body. Counts that had ranged from 25 to 232 raw fragments fell to a median of one relevant component and a maximum of four. Raising the mutation rate did not reveal hidden fission events. Between mutation rates of 0.1 and 1.0, 62–83 per cent of snapshots instead hit the detector’s 256-component ceiling, usually because mutation had dissolved the organism into speckle.

Under these conditions, fragmentation, striping and dust were much easier to produce than a clean physical division, and calling those artifacts “fission” would have contaminated everything measured afterward. We therefore left the predictive-information outputs unopened, abandoned spontaneous fission as the entry point for this campaign, and moved to imposed growth-and-division cycles whose events could be identified without guesswork.

Later experiments kept the distinction between low-density fraying and physical division.
