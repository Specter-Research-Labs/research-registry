# Why the same disturbance matters less later in development

_16–29 August 2026 · Developmental perturbation study._

We often describe an organism as becoming more stable as it develops, but size alone can create the same appearance. A fixed disturbance spread across a larger body may simply be more dilute. To distinguish growth from a deeper change in organization, we fixed the experiment before opening the outcomes and applied matched disturbances to younger and older members of 16 Flow Lenia families.

The disturbance touched about 12 per cent of the organism’s matter at both ages. Even with that proportion held steady, its immediate effect was 91 per cent smaller in the older organisms.

We then increased the dose until the younger and older bodies showed a comparable initial disruption. The older bodies required 7.7 times as much. Development had changed the body’s susceptibility: the matched intervention no longer had the same power to alter its state.

A companion experiment asked whether a controller could exploit a view of the whole organism to steer it better than a local strategy could. While control was active, the whole-body controller outperformed leaving the organism alone. Once “do nothing” was included among the strategies available for each individual case, however, whole-body control was not reliably better than the safest local alternative. At 128 steps after control ended, its advantage had reversed. The controller could force a stronger short-term correction without leaving behind the most durable recovery.

The final tests asked whether the response belonged to the organism's developed internal state rather than to its visible outline alone. Mature organisms in different internal states received the same probe. We then transplanted internal state between organisms to see whether the recipient's response moved toward that of the donor. Independent replay reproduced the analysis exactly. These experiments suggest that internal state carried information about how the body would respond, although they addressed a different question from age-related resistance.

Taken together, the results show that development changed more than body size. Older organisms were much harder to perturb immediately, while stronger whole-body control bought a better short-term correction at the cost of poorer long-term recovery. Whether internal state can predict or transfer those responses remains a separate, promising question.
