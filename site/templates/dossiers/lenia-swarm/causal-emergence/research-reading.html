Two Flow Lenia bodies can look identical and still develop differently, because the visible shape does not reveal all of their internal composition. We also find that a disturbance which redirects an early body has much less influence later in development. If we want to know how to change a body, we need to understand what makes these possibilities available at one moment and harder to reach at another.

The three articles below consolidate a larger experimental record around distinct questions. Later controls sit beside the claims they test, and a failed prediction remains part of the answer. The underlying reports preserve methods, individual runs, and earlier interpretations.

What connects the findings

Changing a body’s internal composition can send its development elsewhere, but transplanting younger composition into an older body did not establish a restored response to the challenge we were testing. Whatever makes the younger body susceptible seems to depend on how that composition works within the receiving state. Finding that dependence would bring us closer to explaining how development changes what we can do to a body.

The prediction and control tests supply the practical standard. A recovery score did not establish predictive improvement, and whole-state feedback achieved targets without establishing the proposed advantage over restricted alternatives. An explanation becomes more useful when it tells us what an intervention will do before we observe its result.

The conjecture to test

A map organized by responses might connect bodies that look different while separating bodies whose images look alike. That could join the existing morphospace to a measured account of susceptibility: which changes remain available, which disturbances are absorbed, and which effects persist after intervention ends. It remains a research direction. Its test is prediction on new interventions and new families, with the delivered disturbance distinguished from the dynamics that follow it.
