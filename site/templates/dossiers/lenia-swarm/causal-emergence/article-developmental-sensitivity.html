Less easily diverted, without growing more alike

If a developing body becomes harder to divert, do nearby starting states also become more likely to reach the same form? We measured both changes in the same cohort. Applying the same local swapping procedure at later ages produced less change 120 steps afterward, even as patterns grown from varied initial conditions became more different from one another. In this comparison, development made the bodies less sensitive to that disturbance without bringing their forms together.

Before interpreting the smaller response as resistance, we have to know how much the intervention actually disturbed the body, because swapping the same fraction of neighboring cells can move less matter in an older state. A later experiment therefore recorded the matter moved and adjusted the swap fraction in an attempt to produce the same early change in shape at different ages.

Later bodies still differed less from their untouched counterparts in the adjusted comparison, with the downstream age contrast pointing the same way in all 16 groups. This strengthens the case for a developmental change in sensitivity, although imperfect matching prevents a clean separation of the initial disturbance from what happens afterward. It also matters that the effect was spread across development: the proposed information peak did not identify a special onset of resistance.

There are two different kinds of consistency at stake. Repeated development might keep producing the same form, or each of several different forms might become harder to disturb once established. The coexistence of increasing sibling differences and decreasing response makes the second possibility worth taking seriously. It offers a picture of development in which robustness preserves a diversity of outcomes. The biological concept of canalization concerns robustness of developmental outcomes, although these finite-time measurements do not locate attractors or measure the sizes of their basins.

What the age curve does not explain

The age curve describes the response to one local swapping procedure, leaving open whether an older body would also resist changes to its internal composition or environment. Other experiments in the record find that intervention rankings, and sometimes the sign of an effect, change with age, so what we need is a comparison of several disturbances that can tell us which aspects of the body have become harder to change.

A separate fresh-cohort confirmation did not establish the earlier fine-shape effect attributed to information-directed intervention. That result belongs beside the positive age trend: a reproducible change with development does not guarantee that the proposed information coordinate explains or controls it.

What has become harder to change?

The working idea is that development changes which disturbances can redirect a body. A rearrangement that once altered its later form may lose that influence, even while another intervention remains effective. This would make developmental commitment a property of the relationship between a body and a disturbance: a mature form could be resistant along one route and readily changeable along another. The age curves motivate that interpretation, and the composition and transplant experiments begin to ask what those different routes consist of.
