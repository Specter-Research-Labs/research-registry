An unchanged picture, a different future

We can ask how much a picture tells us about a body by changing something the picture cannot show: at each location, two material channels contribute to the visible field, so reallocating matter between them can leave that field unchanged while altering its internal composition. Across 64 specimens, these hidden rewrites changed later size and trajectory, even though the saved starting-state audit records identical visible fields and preserved overall channel balance. Because the update rule distinguishes the channels, a change in their allocation can affect the future even when their sum stays fixed. The more demanding question is whether a particular allocation carries a response that we can transfer to another state.

The initial experiment leaves a problem: a decoy rewrite also changes the future, so divergence alone does not tell us what matters about the arrangement. The later spatial experiments go further by taking apart the pattern of channel allocation, asking which parts can be scrambled or removed while retaining a particular developmental effect.

Expansion and contraction depend on different arrangements

For 39 specimens, the experiment pairs a compact and an expansive state, then puts the donor’s channel proportions into the recipient while retaining the recipient’s visible density. The question is whether its later morphology moves toward the donor’s own continuation. To find out what carries that influence, we compare the intact transplant with versions whose broad spatial pattern has been removed or whose Fourier phases have been scrambled. The edited patterns have the same measured strength of change from the host, allowing us to compare their arrangements without simply making one intervention larger.

Removing the broad pattern weakens the expansion-directed effect, while the fine structure still carries a positive contraction-directed effect. Full phase scrambling gives a similar contrast in the averages, leaving expansion near zero and contraction positive, although the uncertainty on the expansion comparison is wider. This suggests that making a body expand and making it contract need different spatial relationships, even when their overall strength is matched. We can begin to ask what the pattern is doing, because we have changes that selectively interfere with one outcome.

Calling this a developmental “code” would mean more than giving each arrangement a name. We would want to know which relationships can be substituted, which must be retained, and whether they have the same effect in another body. The later transfer study already tests that last possibility using shared rearrangements across families; it does not establish a generally useful choice for both expansion and contraction. The interesting possibility is that a body’s geometry and dynamics help determine what an internal pattern can make it do.

The matched-strength spatial experiment · The initial hidden-composition experiment

A biological example gives this question a concrete counterpart. In zebrafish neural progenitors, an elongated cell can retain an influence on its daughters’ eventual fates after rounding up for division. Akanuma and colleagues connected this history to the distribution of Delta protein through imaging, shape manipulation, and modeling. For our artificial bodies, the corresponding ambition is to follow how an arrangement acquires an effect and what subsequently carries it, including when the visible form has changed.

Can younger composition make an older body responsive again?

A reciprocal transplant separates the age of the receiving body from the age of its hidden organization. In 64 specimens, the experiment places age-48 composition into an age-32 body and age-32 composition into an age-48 body, preserving the recipient’s visible matter and overall channel balance according to the saved transplant audit. It then applies the same sequence-based challenge to each constructed state.

The challenge compares opposite orders of pulses along a direction defined earlier in development, with an orthogonal direction as a control. Its measured response is the difference between these order effects on radius. This is a test of susceptibility to a particular intervention, not a measure of whether a body is responsive to anything at all.

Putting older composition into the early recipient left a positive response, whereas younger composition did not establish a restored response in the later recipient. The direct factorial comparison supports a recipient-age effect in this immediate window while leaving the hidden-age effect unresolved, so this test supports an effect of the receiving state without resolving how much the donor’s age contributes.

The transplant changes only part of the state. Its result is consistent with the idea that a channel arrangement has different effects in different recipients, but it does not yet identify that interaction. Redirecting a trajectory and transferring a specified susceptibility are distinct outcomes, and this comparison tests the second.

Inspect the reciprocal transplant and its other time windows. The spatial experiment above and this age transplant ask complementary questions: what must be preserved in a pattern for an effect to survive, and what must be present in the receiving body for that pattern to work?

Developmental biologists call the ability to respond to a particular cue competence. In McConnell and Kaznowski’s cortical transplantation experiments, whether young progenitors adopted the fate appropriate to an older host depended on their stage in the cell cycle. Frantz and McConnell later found that late progenitors retained an upper-layer fate even in younger hosts producing deep-layer neurons. These experiments make developmental state part of the explanation of what a cue can accomplish. Our transplant changes channel composition rather than moving cells between brains, but it asks a related question about which part of a developing system makes a response possible.
